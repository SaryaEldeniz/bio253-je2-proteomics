Interactions between bacterial pathogens can alter physiology and persistence in chronic infections. Pseudomonas aeruginosa releases compounds that reshape Staphylococcus aureus metabolism, while the kinase Stk1 and phosphatase Stp1 regulate staphylococcal physiology through protein phosphorylation. However, their contribution to protein responses elicited by this supernatant remains incompletely resolved. Here we show that Stk1 and Stp1 disruption is associated with distinct metabolic protein responses to this chemical environment. We analysed triplicate total-protein and phosphosite measurements from Staphylococcus aureus JE2 and two transposon mutants in control broth and after prolonged exposure to cell-free Pseudomonas aeruginosa supernatant, using genotype-by-environment contrasts, functional enrichment and association networks. Analyses excluding estimates imputed from detection limits implicated purine metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, nitrogen metabolism and transfer RNA charging at a false discovery rate below 0.1. The kinase mutant showed a stronger decline in purine-biosynthetic protein abundance and a larger increase in the abundance of respiratory proteins than the parent strain. Purine enrichment was supported by both rank-based and overrepresentation analyses, and protein responses related to nitrogen metabolism and transfer RNA charging also differed between genotypes. IsaA, Pyk and QoxB overlapped between the selected protein network and mutant-associated phosphosite candidates, linking proteins involved in cell wall remodelling and energy metabolism across the two datasets. However, these significant phosphosite comparisons concerned control broth, and none of the corresponding sites showed a significant genotype-dependent response to supernatant. Reduced Stk1 abundance in the phosphatase mutant further limits enzyme-specific interpretation. These findings associate Stk1/Stp1 disruption with metabolic proteome remodelling and prioritise candidates for protein-adjusted phosphosite analysis and functional tests of responses to bacterial secreted products.
